## Valuation, Sensitivity & Scenarios

**The three accounting worlds.** The model presents Unilever's Argentina and Türkiye exposure under three accounting treatments. **World A** is the plain current-rate method with no inflation restatement. Historical-cost plant is translated at a collapsing closing rate, so it shrinks toward nothing in euro terms: the "disappearing plant" baseline. **World B** is the US GAAP temporal method, or remeasurement. Non-monetary items stay at historical FX, monetary items move to current FX, and the difference runs through income as a *remeasurement* gain or loss. **World C** is what Unilever actually reports under IFRS: IAS 29 restatement in local currency, followed by IAS 21 translation of everything at the closing rate. All three describe the same underlying economics under different accounting. They are not three valuations of the business, and this model does not attempt a DCF or multiples valuation.

**The numbers.** For 2024, World A and World B both show revenue of about €60,428m and operating profit of about €8,490m. World C shows revenue of €60,845m and operating profit of €8,496m. Total assets are €46,006m in World A, €46,108m in World B and €46,545m in World C. The net monetary line is nil in World A, a €272m remeasurement loss in World B, and a €195m IAS 29 net monetary loss in World C. The pattern is the one flagged in the model. World C adds about €417m of revenue and €539m of total assets compared with World A, but only about €6m of operating profit. Restating revenue and costs by the same inflation factor largely cancels at the profit line, even though the gross revenue and asset figures move a great deal.

**IAS 29 impact by subsidiary.** In 2024, restatement added €474m of total assets, €230m of turnover and €10m of operating profit in Argentina, with a €206m net monetary loss. In Türkiye it added €65m of total assets and €187m of turnover but *reduced* operating profit by €4m, and it produced an €11m net monetary gain. Argentina's result goes against the textbook heuristic. The model's solved Argentine balance sheet is in a net monetary *liability* position, which the heuristic says should produce a gain, yet it books a loss. The monetary plug also nets against growth in restated opening equity and operating profit, and a fast-growing restated base can outweigh the real erosion of the liabilities. The simple rule is a starting intuition, not a formula that holds once the whole balance sheet is restated together.

**2025 validation.** The 2024 calibration was rolled forward to 2025 without re-fitting, and the result is only a partial match. For both subsidiaries, turnover, operating profit and net monetary gain/loss all have the same sign as Unilever's disclosures. Argentina's figures are −€31m, −€1m and −€24m in the model against −€90m, −€54m and −€46m disclosed. Türkiye's are −€47m, −€3m and −€113m against −€16m, −€46m and −€10m. The model undershoots both operating-profit lines and overshoots Türkiye's net monetary line roughly tenfold. Total assets do **not** match in sign: the model shows +€184m and +€46m, while Unilever disclosed −€199m and −€20m. This gap is structural. Restating non-monetary assets under a rising price index can only increase them, and World A and World C both use the same closing rate. The model's total-assets impact therefore cannot turn negative, and the disclosed negatives probably reflect disposals, impairments or a comparative-basis convention that the summary table does not show.

**Peer comparison.** Other companies disclose the same IAS 29 mechanics. Coca-Cola FEMSA reported an H1 2025 net monetary position gain of Ps.154m (up from Ps.42m), with the increase driven mainly by Argentine liabilities, a textbook case of liabilities eroded by inflation. BBVA's Türkiye (Garanti) net monetary loss of €2,118m in 2023 was partly offset by a separately disclosed €1,202m inflation-linked-bond gain, a hedge this model does not represent. The accounting regime also affects what gets disclosed. Colgate-Palmolive reports under US GAAP, the regime behind World B. It describes the effect of its highly inflationary designations as immaterial, past and expected, and discloses no dollar figure. That is the same "immaterial" conclusion this model reaches for Unilever (a €195m loss, about 2.3% of operating profit), reached independently under a different regime. Reckitt Benckiser, an IFRS filer like Unilever, reports the effect only inside a combined "exchange and hyperinflation" revenue item (£394m in 2025, £24m in 2024) that cannot be separated. Not every IFRS filer discloses at Unilever's level of detail.
