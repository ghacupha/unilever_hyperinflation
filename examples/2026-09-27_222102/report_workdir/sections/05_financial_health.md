## Financial Health

**Overall Grade: C**

The grade comes from a simple, documented rule: each subsidiary's net monetary gain/(loss) is divided by its restated (IAS 29) total assets, and the absolute value of that ratio is mapped to a letter grade (Argentina C, Türkiye A). The overall grade of C matches the weaker of the two. It measures one thing, how exposed each balance sheet is to purchasing-power erosion. It is not a broader credit or solvency score.

| Subsidiary | Sub-grade | Net monetary gain/(loss) | Total assets | Exposure ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Argentina | **C** | €(206.0)m | €1,124.9m | −18.3% |
| Türkiye | **A** | €11.0m | €419.8m | +2.6% |

Argentina alone sets the overall grade. Its net monetary loss of €206.0m equals roughly 18% of its total assets. The loss is not the textbook result of holding excess cash and receivables: Argentina sits in a net monetary *liability* position, and the loss arises because the monetary plug nets against the inflation-driven growth of restated opening equity and operating profit. Because the subsidiary's monetary-asset/liability split is a modeling choice rather than a Unilever disclosure, this says nothing about how management actually runs Argentina's balance sheet. It is the subsidiary whose working-capital and funding mix investors should watch. Türkiye's small €11.0m gain against €419.8m of assets means its net monetary position barely moves under inflation.

These figures are for **2024**, the primary calibrated year. That is the year whose local-currency inputs were solved to reproduce Unilever's disclosed IAS 29 figures. The 2025 roll-forward is only a directional check, not a snapshot of the current position. The subsidiary balance sheets are also an illustrative reconstruction, not Unilever's own disclosure.
